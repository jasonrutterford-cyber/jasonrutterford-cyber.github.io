Last week I used AI to do in an afternoon what used to take a team a fortnight. I gave it the research we had and asked it to work out what people struggle with, what they need, and where the opportunities are. It did. Clean, structured, every problem tied to a next step.

<!--more-->

The feedback was fair. The problems it found were generic. Not detailed enough to build anything on.

Of course they were. The AI did exactly what it was asked. What went in was thin, because nobody had spent real time with real people. What came out couldn't be any richer than that.

## Where the detail comes from

It comes from sitting with people while they do the job. Watching the step where they sigh, or open a spreadsheet on the side because the system doesn't do what they need. Asking why, in person. Hearing the same thing from enough people that you know it's a pattern and not an opinion.

AI can't do that. It can't spend a morning next to someone. It can only take what that morning produced and do useful things with it: sort it, connect it, write it up. That part is now fast and nearly free.

But it's the second half of the job. If the first half hasn't happened, AI doesn't fill the gap. It covers it, and it does so confidently. A generic finding written well looks the same as a real one, right up until you build on it.

## The mistake I keep seeing

Not that people trust AI too much. That people assume we can now move faster, full stop.

We can. But the speed comes with a caveat that gets lost between the demo and the plan: the detail is high level. It's as good as the time spent with people underneath it. Skip that, and you get the same shallow answer you'd have got before, just sooner.

## What this means for your budget

If you're told "AI will do the research", ask what it's researching. If the answer is "the documents we already have", you'll get back what you already know, tidied up.

AI can speed up the process. But don't spend money on it if you haven't got the level of insight to deliver a quality outcome. You pay twice: once for the AI, and again when the thing built on a generic problem doesn't land.

The cheaper order is the old one. A small amount of time with real people first: a handful of them, watched doing the job, asked why. Then let AI do what it's good at with that material.

## One question to ask

When someone shows you findings that AI produced, ask: who did we sit with, and for how long?

If the answer is nobody, you don't have findings yet. You have a summary. Fund the sitting-with. The AI part is nearly free.
